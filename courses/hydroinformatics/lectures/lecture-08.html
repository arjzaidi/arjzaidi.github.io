1. From rainfall to runoff

Runoff models start from a simple question: how much of the rain that falls on a catchment actually reaches the outlet, and when? This section sets up the rainfall–runoff relationship that the models in the rest of the lecture quantify.

From gross rainfall to effective rainfall

Definition

Effective rainfall is the portion of rainfall that produces a perceptible streamflow response. It equals the gross precipitation measured by a gauge, minus the losses.

TermDescription
Gross precipitationRain measured by a gauge.
Interception & infiltrationLosses and storage before runoff occurs.
Effective rainfallThe portion contributing to streamflow.
Effective precipitation = gross precipitation − losses

Not all rainfall becomes surface runoff; some infiltrates, some is stored, and some contributes to subsurface flow.

Infiltration and runoff generation

  • Dry soil may initially have a high infiltration capacity.
  • If rainfall intensity is below infiltration capacity, little or no overland flow occurs — although interflow may still develop.
  • With continuing rainfall, the soil becomes wetter and infiltration capacity can decline.
  • When rainfall exceeds the capacity of the soil to absorb water, the excess water contributes to runoff.

The catchment as a rainfall–runoff system

A catchment can be treated as a system that transforms rainfall input into streamflow output:

  • Inputs include rainfall amount, intensity, duration and spatial distribution.
  • The catchment controls the transformation through soils, land cover, slope, drainage and storage.
  • The observed output is discharge at the catchment outlet.
Rainfall input → Catchment processes → Runoff transformation → Discharge output
  • Losses and storage control how much rainfall becomes effective rainfall.
  • Catchment routing controls when that water reaches the outlet.

2. Hydrograph analysis

Definition

A hydrograph is discharge plotted against time at a channel location. Its three parts are the rising limb, the peak and the recession.

Discharge ^ | ,-. | ,' `. | ,' `---..__ | ,' |____,'_______________________> Time rising peak recession limb

Reading a hydrograph

  • The hydrologic response of rainfall at the outlet of an area is recorded as a hydrograph: discharge (cubic feet per second) in a channel versus time.
  • The area under the curve yields the volume of runoff.
  • Streamflow = Direct Runoff + Base flow.
  • Direct runoff (DRO) = rainfall excess = rainfall − losses.
  • Losses include interception, infiltration and depression storage — sometimes called basin recharge.

How catchment characteristics change the response

  • Steeper slopes generally produce faster runoff and a sharper hydrograph response.
  • Impervious or highly developed surfaces can reduce infiltration and increase rapid runoff.
  • Larger catchments generally produce greater runoff volume for the same rainfall depth, but their response is often broader and slower.
  • Vegetation, soil properties, channel length, drainage density, and natural/channel storage also modify the response.
  • Snowmelt at high elevation can produce broader, lower-peaked flood responses than intense rainfall.
Same monthly rainfall, different risk

Two months can record the same total rainfall but carry very different runoff risk. What decision changes when the hydrograph changes?

Case study — the 2013 Sindh floods

The 2013 floods in Sindh illustrate how a large volume of rainfall translated into a broad, damaging flood wave across the lower Indus plain — a reminder that peak timing and volume depend on both watershed and storm conditions.

3. Rainfall–runoff modeling

Objective

Rainfall–runoff modeling estimates the runoff or streamflow response produced by a rainfall event or rainfall time series.

  • Inputs may include rainfall, soil properties, land cover, topography and antecedent conditions.
  • Observed discharge is used for calibration and evaluation where measurements are available.
  • Model choice depends on the purpose, data availability, catchment scale, and required level of detail.

Rainfall data — gauge and satellite are complements

SourceStrengthsLimitations
Rain gaugeDirect point measurement; long local records where maintained.Sparse coverage; missing values; wind/exposure errors.
SatelliteSpatially complete, near-real-time products.Bias in mountains and intense localized storms; validate locally.

Choosing a rainfall–runoff method

The method should match the decision, the watershed scale, and the available data:

MethodWhat it estimates
Rational MethodEstimates peak discharge for suitable small catchments — not event volume.
Curve Number MethodEstimates event runoff depth from rainfall, soil and land-cover conditions.
Hydrological & hydraulic (H&H) modelingCan simulate runoff timing and routing when suitable data are available.

Use local evidence and check assumptions before accepting results.

Regression or machine learning as a hydrologic analysis tool

  • Regression can relate runoff or streamflow to explanatory variables when a suitable data record is available.
  • Regression works best for long time periods.
  • Possible predictors include rainfall characteristics, basin descriptors, or other hydrometeorological variables.
  • Regression is an empirical relationship; it should be evaluated using appropriate goodness-of-fit and validation measures.

Can we trust the model results?

A good-looking result is not enough

Without evidence, a plausible hydrograph can still be wrong. Model results should be checked, not assumed.

  • Check rainfall depth, timing and spatial coverage.
  • Compare simulated and observed flood peaks, timing and volume.
  • Test reasonable ranges of uncertain parameters.
  • Validate with another event or independent field evidence.
  • Report uncertainty rather than relying on one number.

4. Two families of runoff models

Runoff models estimate the streamflow a watershed will produce. They fall into two broad families, depending on what they predict:

FamilyWhat it provides
Peak runoff modelsProvide only estimates of peak discharge from the watershed.
Continuous runoff modelsProvide storm hydrographs for a given rainfall hyetograph — an estimate of runoff versus time.
Remember

A peak runoff model answers “how high will the flood peak be?” A continuous runoff model answers “what will the whole hydrograph look like, and when?”

5. Peak runoff models — the Rational Method

The Rational Method is a deterministic, lumped model — also called the Lloyd-Davies method (circa 1850). It estimates the peak discharge from a small catchment.

Assumptions

  • The catchment is small (less than about 200 acres).
  • The catchment is concentrated — that is, rainfall duration is longer than or at least equal to the time of concentration of the watershed.
  • Rainfall intensity is uniform over the area of study.
  • The runoff coefficient is a catch-all coefficient that incorporates all losses of the catchment.

The Rational Method formula

Formula

Qp = C · Ii · A

SymbolMeaning
QpPeak discharge (cfs).
CRunoff coefficient — a function of soil type, land use/cover and watershed slope. It is the ratio of the total runoff rate to the precipitation, and is dimensionless.
IiAverage rainfall intensity (in/hr) for a storm duration equal to the time of concentration, Tc.
AArea (acre).

The rainfall intensity for a given duration and return period is read from intensity–duration–frequency (IDF) curves. Runoff coefficients can be looked up in the standard references (e.g. Bedient Table 6-5, McCuen).

Runoff coefficient — key details of the reference table

ParameterCovered values
Land use / coverAgricultural, residential, commercial, industrial, or open/natural covers such as grasslands and forests.
Hydrologic soil groups (A, B, C, D)Soils classified by infiltration and transmission rates — from high-infiltration sands and gravels (Group A) to low-infiltration clays (Group D).
Slope rangesGeneral gradient classes (flat, rolling, steep) that affect the velocity and volume of surface runoff.
ApplicationThe table values are empirical runoff coefficients (C) used in peak-discharge equations.

Runoff coefficient for a non-homogeneous area

When a drainage basin has different runoff potentials, it is subdivided and a weighted runoff coefficient is computed based on the area of each land use:

Formula

Cw = Σj=1n (Cj · Aj) / Σj=1n Aj

C reflects the effect of land use, soil and slope on runoff potential, assuming the watershed is homogeneous. If the drainage basin has different runoff potentials, it is subdivided and each part is weighted by its area.

6. The NRCS Curve Number method

The NRCS Curve Number method was developed by the USA Soil Conservation Service (now the Natural Resources Conservation Service, NRCS) of the USDA to predict peak discharge due to a 24-hour storm event.

  • It uses empirically derived relationships that combine precipitation, land cover, hydrologic soil group and watershed characteristics to estimate runoff depth.
  • When combined with a unit hydrograph method, it can also be used to estimate peak discharge and runoff hydrographs.
  • It is a more sophisticated approach than the Rational Method, and can be used for drainage areas up to about 2,000 acres.

The curve number

The curve number (CN) is a coefficient that reduces the total precipitation to runoff potential, after “losses”:

  • Evaporation, absorption and transpiration
  • Surface storage
  • The higher the CN value, the higher the runoff potential.
  • It is essential to use the CN value that best mimics the ground-cover type and hydrologic condition.
  • A CN of 100 is used for permanent water surfaces such as lakes and ponds.

The NRCS rainfall–runoff equation

Equation 1

Q = (P − Ia)² / [ (P − Ia) + S ]

SymbolMeaning
QAccumulated direct runoff (inches or mm).
PAccumulated rainfall — the potential maximum runoff (inches or mm).
IaInitial abstraction — surface storage, interception, evaporation and infiltration prior to any runoff occurring (inches or mm).
SPotential maximum soil-moisture retention after runoff begins (inches or mm).
Important

For P ≤ Ia, Q = 0. If (P − Ia) is negative, there is no runoff.

Potential maximum retention (S)

S is calculated from the curve number:

Equation 2

S = (z / CN) − z    where z = 10 for English units, or 254 for metric units, and CN = runoff curve number (0–100).

Generally the initial abstraction is estimated as:

Equation 3

Ia = 0.2 S

Substituting Equation 3 into Equation 1 gives the commonly used form:

Equation 4

Q = (P − 0.2 S)² / (P + 0.8 S)

Equation 2 was developed mainly for small watersheds from recorded storm data that included total daily rainfall but not its distribution over time. The method is therefore appropriate for estimating direct runoff from 24-hour or one-day storm rainfall.

Curve number — what controls it

The principal physical watershed characteristics affecting the relationship between rainfall and runoff are:

  • Land use and land treatment
  • Soil types
  • Land slope

The NRCS method uses a combination of soil conditions and land uses (ground cover) to assign a runoff factor to an area (CN). Soils have differing rates of infiltration, so the NRCS divides them into four hydrologic soil groups.

Hydrologic soil groups

GroupInfiltration & runoffTypical soilsInfiltration rate (wet)
AHigh infiltration (low runoff)Sand, loamy sand, or sandy loam> 0.3 in/hr
BModerate infiltration (moderate runoff)Silt loam or loam0.15 – 0.3 in/hr
CLow infiltration (moderate to high runoff)Sandy clay loam0.05 – 0.15 in/hr
DVery low infiltration (high runoff)Clay loam, silty clay loam, sandy clay, silty clay, or clay0 – 0.05 in/hr
  • Effects of urbanization: consider the effect of urbanization on the natural hydrologic soil group. For compacted soil, make appropriate changes to the selected soil group.

Antecedent moisture conditions (AMC)

Antecedent moisture is the preceding relative moisture of the pervious surfaces prior to the rainfall event:

  • Low when there has been little preceding rainfall.
  • High when there has been considerable preceding rainfall.
ClassCondition
AMC IDry
AMC IIAverage — recommended for most hydrologic analysis
AMC IIIWet — may be used for areas with high water-table conditions

Composite curve number

When a drainage area has more than one land use, the area is treated as a single uniform land use represented by the composite curve number, calculated by the weighted method:

Formula

CNcomposite = Σ (CNj · Aj) / Σ Aj

Worked example

Problem

A 100-acre catchment has a curve number CN = 75. A storm delivers P = 4.0 inches of rain under average moisture conditions (AMC II). Estimate the direct runoff Q using the NRCS Curve Number method.

Step 1 — Compute the potential maximum retention S.

S = (1000 / CN) − 10 = (1000 / 75) − 10 = 13.33 − 10 = 3.33 inches.

Step 2 — Compute the initial abstraction Ia.

Ia = 0.2 S = 0.2 × 3.33 = 0.67 inches. Since P = 4.0 in > Ia, runoff occurs.

Step 3 — Compute the direct runoff Q.

Q = (P − 0.2S)² / (P + 0.8S) = (4.0 − 0.67)² / (4.0 + 0.8×3.33) = (3.33)² / (4.0 + 2.67) = 11.11 / 6.67 = ≈ 1.67 inches of direct runoff.

Only about 1.67 of the 4.0 inches becomes direct runoff — the rest is abstracted by interception, surface storage, evaporation and infiltration. Repeating the calculation with a higher CN (say 90) would give a much larger Q for the same rainfall.

7. Continuous runoff models

Continuous runoff models produce a full hydrograph rather than a single peak value. The lecture covers the time-area method and unit hydrograph techniques.

The time-area method

  • Developed to address non-uniform rainfall over large areas.
  • Converts rainfall excess into a hydrograph using the concept of a time-area histogram.
  • Assumes the outflow hydrograph results from pure translation of direct runoff to the outlet at uniform velocity, ignoring any storage effects in the watershed.
  • The watershed is divided into subareas with distinct runoff translation times to the outlet.
  • Subareas are delineated with isochrones of equal translation time, numbered upstream from the outlet.

Unit hydrograph techniques

  • A unit hydrograph is the direct-runoff hydrograph produced by one unit of rainfall excess (for example, 1 inch or 1 cm) falling uniformly over the catchment in a specified duration.
  • Once the unit hydrograph is known, it can be scaled and combined to build the storm hydrograph for a rainfall of different depth or duration.
  • Together with the NRCS Curve Number method, unit hydrograph techniques allow the estimation of both peak discharge and the runoff hydrograph.

Key takeaways

  • Only part of the rainfall becomes runoff: effective rainfall = gross rainfall − losses, and runoff is generated when rainfall exceeds the soil’s infiltration capacity.
  • A catchment transforms rainfall input into streamflow output — losses and storage control how much becomes runoff, and routing controls when it arrives.
  • The hydrograph (rising limb, peak, recession) records the catchment response; the area under the curve is the runoff volume, and its shape reflects both storm and catchment characteristics.
  • Rainfall–runoff modeling requires appropriate data, clear assumptions, calibration and validation — a plausible hydrograph is not proof of a correct model.
  • Runoff models fall into two families: peak runoff models (peak discharge only) and continuous runoff models (a full runoff versus time hydrograph).
  • The Rational Method Qp = C·Ii·A is a lumped peak-discharge model for small, concentrated catchments — it estimates peak flow, not event volume.
  • The runoff coefficient C captures soil, land use/cover and slope; a non-homogeneous area needs a weighted C by area.
  • The NRCS Curve Number method estimates event runoff depth from Q = (P − 0.2S)² / (P + 0.8S), with S = (z / CN) − z.
  • The higher the CN, the higher the runoff potential; CN depends on land use, soil (hydrologic soil group A–D), slope and antecedent moisture (AMC I/II/III).
  • Multiple land uses are combined into a composite CN; the same weighting idea applies to the Rational method’s runoff coefficient.
  • Continuous models — the time-area method (isochrones, pure translation, no storage) and unit hydrograph techniques — turn rainfall excess into a hydrograph.
