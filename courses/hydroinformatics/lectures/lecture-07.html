1. Rainfall analysis

Rainfall is the primary atmospheric input to a watershed, but it varies in time and space, so a single gauge may not represent an entire catchment. Rainfall analysis describes that variability and prepares the input for rainfall–runoff analysis.

Mean rainfall

MeasureMeaning
Mean annual rainfallAverage annual rainfall over a long period — roughly 30–40 years of records.
Mean monthly rainfallAverage rainfall for a given month over multiple years.

Comparing observed annual rainfall with the long-term mean helps identify years with a rainfall deficit or surplus. Use a sufficiently long and continuous record when characterizing climatological rainfall.

Rainfall characteristics

  • Rainfall varies in time and space, so a single gauge may not represent an entire catchment.
  • Storm intensity, duration and spatial distribution are especially important for runoff generation.
  • Rainfall analysis provides the input information for rainfall–runoff analysis.

From gross rainfall to effective rainfall

Definition

Effective rainfall is the portion of rainfall that produces a perceptible streamflow response. It equals the gross precipitation measured by a gauge, minus the losses.

TermDescription
Gross precipitationRain measured by a gauge.
Interception & infiltrationLosses and storage before runoff occurs.
Effective rainfallThe portion contributing to streamflow.
Effective precipitation = gross precipitation − losses

Not all rainfall becomes surface runoff; some infiltrates, some is stored, and some contributes to subsurface flow.

Infiltration and runoff generation

  • Dry soil may initially have a high infiltration capacity.
  • If rainfall intensity is below infiltration capacity, little or no overland flow occurs — although interflow may still develop.
  • With continuing rainfall, the soil becomes wetter and infiltration capacity can decline.
  • When rainfall exceeds the capacity of the soil to absorb water, the excess water contributes to runoff.

2. Watershed concepts

The watershed is the basic spatial unit for hydrologic analysis.

Definition

A watershed is the area that contributes flow to a specified point or outlet. It is a basic hydrologic unit within which measurements, calculations and predictions are made.

  • Watersheds can be very small or can encompass major river basins such as the Indus Basin.
  • Boundaries are commonly defined by topographic divides or ridgelines.
  • To read a watershed from topography, use contour lines and ridges to identify drainage divides — water flows downslope toward channels.

Watershed delineation: the core idea

StepAction
1Select the outlet or pour point.
2Identify the surrounding topographic divide.
3Trace the boundary so that all land inside drains toward the outlet.
4The resulting polygon represents the contributing watershed.

GIS watershed delineation automates this logic using a DEM and flow-direction / flow-accumulation analysis.

From DEM to watershed boundary

A computer-generated boundary should always be checked against the landscape:

  1. Check the DEM and correct important errors.
  2. Identify flow direction and the stream network.
  3. Set the outlet at the actual decision site.
  4. Delineate the watershed and calculate its area.
  5. Verify channels and drainage paths in the field.

What a DEM can — and cannot — tell you

CategoryDetail
Reliable usesDelineation, slope, flow direction, relative relief.
Common errorsRoad embankments, flat areas, sinks, and poorly represented channels.
Field checksOutlet position, channel continuity, and cross-drainage.
Available sourcesSRTM and other DEMs — document resolution and date.

Drainage network

  • A drainage network is the set of cells or channels through which surface water flows.
  • Flow accumulation identifies locations receiving flow from many upstream cells.
  • A stream can be defined by applying a contributing-area threshold.
  • The threshold should reflect the scale and purpose of the analysis — it is not a universal value.

Key basin characteristics

CharacteristicMeaning
Drainage areaArea contributing flow to a common outlet.
Drainage divideBoundary separating watersheds draining to different outlets.
Drainage densityTotal stream length per unit basin area.
Stream frequencyNumber of channels per unit area.
Stream gradientElevation drop per unit channel length.
Basin reliefHighest elevation minus lowest elevation.
Time of concentrationTravel time from the hydraulically most distant point to the outlet.

Stream order — the Strahler system

Stream ordering is a quantitative method for describing the hierarchy of a drainage network (1st through 12th order, after Strahler, 1952):

  • First-order streams are headwater channels with no upstream tributaries of the same order.
  • When two streams of the same order join, the downstream segment increases by one order.
  • When different orders join, the downstream segment retains the higher order.

Strahler’s system is widely used for drainage-network analysis.

3. Water budget & rainfall–runoff

How water moves through a watershed

StageWhat happens
HeadwatersRainfall and snowmelt in the upper areas begin to flow downhill.
TributariesSmall streams collect water from different parts of the watershed and join larger streams.
Main channelsWater flows through the main river or stream, carrying water and sediment downstream.
OutletWater leaves the watershed through a common outlet, such as a river, lake, reservoir or sea.

Basin water balance

Balance termComponents
InflowsPrecipitation, surface-water inflow, groundwater inflow, and artificial imports.
OutflowsEvaporation, runoff, groundwater outflow, and artificial withdrawals.
StorageSurface water, soil moisture, groundwater, snow/ice, and temporary/intercepted storage.
Hydrologic mass balance

Inflow = Outflow ± change in storage   →   Σ Inflows − Σ Outflows = Δ Storage

Rainfall–runoff response

A catchment can be treated as a system that transforms rainfall input into streamflow output:

  • Inputs include rainfall amount, intensity, duration and spatial distribution.
  • The catchment controls the transformation through soils, land cover, slope, drainage and storage.
  • The observed output is discharge at the catchment outlet.

4. Hydrograph analysis

Definition

A hydrograph is discharge plotted against time at a channel location. Its three parts are the rising limb, the peak and the recession.

Discharge ^ | ,-. | ,' `. | ,' `---..__ | ,' |____,'_______________________> Time rising peak recession limb

Hydrograph analysis

  • The hydrologic response of rainfall at the outlet of an area is recorded as a hydrograph: discharge (cubic feet per second) in a channel versus time.
  • The area under the curve yields the volume of runoff.
  • Streamflow = Direct Runoff + Base flow.
  • Direct runoff (DRO) = rainfall excess = rainfall − losses.
  • Losses include interception, infiltration and depression storage — sometimes called basin recharge.

What controls hydrograph shape?

Storm / climateCatchment
Rainfall intensityCatchment size and shape
Rainfall durationSlope and elevation
Rainfall spatial distributionSoil and land cover
Rainfall temporal distributionDrainage density, roughness and storage
 Antecedent moisture

How catchment characteristics change the response

  • Steeper slopes generally produce faster runoff and a sharper hydrograph response.
  • Impervious or highly developed surfaces can reduce infiltration and increase rapid runoff.
  • Larger catchments generally produce greater runoff volume for the same rainfall depth, but their response is often broader and slower.
  • Vegetation, soil properties, channel length, drainage density, and natural/channel storage also modify the response.
  • Snowmelt at high elevation can produce broader, lower-peaked flood responses than intense rainfall.
Same monthly rainfall, different risk

Two months can record the same total rainfall but carry very different runoff risk. What decision changes when the hydrograph changes?

5. Rainfall–runoff modeling

Objective

Rainfall–runoff modeling estimates the runoff or streamflow response produced by a rainfall event or rainfall time series.

  • Inputs may include rainfall, soil properties, land cover, topography and antecedent conditions.
  • Observed discharge is used for calibration and evaluation where measurements are available.
  • Model choice depends on the purpose, data availability, catchment scale, and required level of detail.

A simple conceptual model

Rainfall input → Catchment processes → Runoff transformation → Discharge output
  • Losses and storage control how much rainfall becomes effective rainfall.
  • Catchment routing controls when that water reaches the outlet.

Rainfall data — gauge and satellite are complements

SourceStrengthsLimitations
Rain gaugeDirect point measurement; long local records where maintained.Sparse coverage; missing values; wind/exposure errors.
SatelliteSpatially complete, near-real-time products.Bias in mountains and intense localized storms; validate locally.

Choosing a rainfall–runoff method

The method should match the decision, the watershed scale, and the available data:

MethodWhat it estimates
Rational MethodEstimates peak discharge for suitable small catchments — not event volume.
Curve Number MethodEstimates event runoff depth from rainfall, soil and land-cover conditions.
Hydrological & hydraulic (H&H) modelingCan simulate runoff timing and routing when suitable data are available.

Use local evidence and check assumptions before accepting results.

Regression or machine learning as a hydrologic analysis tool

  • Regression can relate runoff or streamflow to explanatory variables when a suitable data record is available.
  • Regression works best for long time periods.
  • Possible predictors include rainfall characteristics, basin descriptors, or other hydrometeorological variables.
  • Regression is an empirical relationship; it should be evaluated using appropriate goodness-of-fit and validation measures.

Can we trust the model results?

A good-looking result is not enough

Without evidence, a plausible hydrograph can still be wrong. Model results should be checked, not assumed.

  • Check rainfall depth, timing and spatial coverage.
  • Compare simulated and observed flood peaks, timing and volume.
  • Test reasonable ranges of uncertain parameters.
  • Validate with another event or independent field evidence.
  • Report uncertainty rather than relying on one number.

In-class activity

Work with a rainfall event and a hydrograph

Use the sketch of a flood hydrograph and the catchment it drains. Work through the questions below before revealing the answers.

1. Label the rising limb, the peak and the recession limb.

The rising limb is the steeply increasing part as the storm begins; the peak is the maximum discharge; the recession is the slowly falling limb as the catchment drains after the rain stops.

2. Estimate which part of the hydrograph represents direct runoff.

The portion above the baseflow separation line — the rapid response driven by rainfall excess. Baseflow is the slower, sustained contribution from groundwater that continues after the direct runoff has passed.

3. Which catchment characteristics could explain a sharp versus broad response?

Steep slopes, impervious or urbanised surfaces, high drainage density, small size and wet antecedent conditions favour a sharp, flashy response. Gentle slopes, permeable soils, dense vegetation, large size, and significant channel/natural storage favour a broad, subdued response.

4. Sketch how the hydrograph might change if rainfall intensity doubled.

A higher peak that arrives earlier and a steeper rising limb — more water is delivered faster than the soil can absorb. The storm may also produce a larger runoff volume, because a greater share of rainfall exceeds the infiltration capacity.

5. What additional data would you need to build a rainfall–runoff model?

Reliable rainfall (gauge and/or satellite), observed streamflow for calibration and validation, a delineated watershed boundary and area, soil properties and hydrologic soil group, land cover, topography (slope and drainage), and antecedent moisture conditions.

Key takeaways

  • Rainfall is the primary atmospheric input, but only part of it becomes effective rainfall and runoff — the rest is lost to interception, infiltration and storage.
  • The watershed provides the spatial framework for hydrologic analysis; topography controls drainage direction and watershed boundaries.
  • A DEM-derived boundary is a starting point — always check the DEM, set the outlet at the true decision site, and verify channels in the field.
  • Basin characteristics (area, shape, slope, drainage density, relief, time of concentration) and Strahler stream order describe how a catchment is organised.
  • The basin water balance Σ Inflows − Σ Outflows = Δ Storage connects rainfall, runoff and storage.
  • The hydrograph shape reflects both storm characteristics and catchment characteristics.
  • Rainfall–runoff modeling represents the transformation from rainfall input to discharge output; good modeling requires appropriate data, clear assumptions, calibration and validation.
