1. The statistical workflow

StepQuestion
1. AskWhat do we want to understand?
2. CollectWho or what should we measure?
3. DescribeWhat patterns appear in the data?
4. InterpretWhat can we responsibly conclude?

Statistics is the bridge from data to decisions — every analysis starts with a question and ends with a responsible conclusion.

2. Populations and samples

TermDefinition
PopulationAn individual or group that represents all the members of a certain group or category of interest
SampleA subset drawn from the larger population
ParameterA value that is either derived from, or applied to, population data
StatisticA value derived from sample data

Example: average income of faculty

Suppose we want to know the average income of the current full-time, tenured faculty at USPCAS-W. One way: get a list of every full-time tenured faculty member and find each annual income — because this list contains every member of the group of interest, it is a population, and the mean we calculate is a parameter. Another way: randomly select a subset of faculty names and average their incomes — this subset is a sample (a random sample), and the mean we generate is a statistic.

Few things to remember

  • A population does not need to be large. If we want the average height of the students in this class, all of you comprise a population. A population is the collection of people, animals, locations, etc. that the study focuses on — e.g. Ford vehicles assembled in the United States during 2005.
  • Samples are not necessarily good representations of the populations from which they were selected. When such differences exist, it is difficult to apply results generated from a sample to the larger population.

3. Sampling strategies

StrategyMeaningExample
Random samplingThe likelihood of selection of each member of the population is equal — “random” does not mean haphazardPick any streamflow value from a population
Stratified samplingThe population is divided into groups, and then random sampling is used within each groupPick a streamflow value from the annual maximum series
Uniform samplingData are selected such that the points are uniformly far apart in time or spacePick streamflow values measured on Monday midnight
Convenience samplingData are collected according to the convenience of the experimenterPick streamflow during summer

Benefit of random sampling: although randomly selected samples may differ from the larger population in important ways (especially if the sample is small), these differences are due to chance rather than to a systematic bias in the selection process. Representative sampling aims to make the sample look similar to the population on some important variables.

4. Describing data

Distributions

A distribution shows the shape of data. A frequency table counts how often values occur; a histogram turns those counts into a visible pattern. When looking at a distribution, look for:

  • Center
  • Spread
  • Shape
  • Unusual values

Some values of a random variable occur more frequently than others. For example, heights of university students around 5’7" are much more common than heights around 4’ or 7’. The frequency distribution shows which values are most common (where the curve is highest). In a probability distribution, the area under the curve between two values a and b is the probability that the random variable takes on values between a and b.

Three ways to describe the center

MeasureMeaning
MeanThe arithmetic average — sum ÷ count
MedianThe middle value when the data is ordered
ModeThe most frequent value

The best measure depends on the shape of the data.

Watch the outlier

Slide the outlier in — and watch the mean move. With 6 values the mean is 7.5; add one outlier (40) and the mean jumps to 12.14. The mean is sensitive to extreme values.

Problem using mean values: the mean alone tells us nothing about the highest or lowest score in the distribution, and nothing about the variety of the scores. That is why we also need measures of variability.

Measures of variability

MeasureMeaning
RangeMeasure of total spread (maximum − minimum). Can be misleading: if 1 student scores 1, another scores 20, and the other 98 all score 10, the range is 19 even though the scores are not really spread out.
VarianceStatistical average of the amount of dispersion in a distribution — the average squared deviation of each score from the mean: sum of (score − mean)² divided by n − 1.
Standard deviationThe square root of the variance — the average difference from the mean.
CovarianceHow two variables vary together (covered in Section 6).

Why square the deviations? Why n − 1?

  • Why square? Squaring removes the sign of the deviations so they do not cancel out, and gives more weight to large deviations.
  • Why n − 1? Because the sample mean usually differs from the population mean, the variance and standard deviation calculated using the sample mean will probably be smaller than they would have been with the population mean — we would underestimate the true population variance. Using n − 1 in the denominator adjusts for this underestimation.

When combined, the mean and standard deviation provide a pretty good picture of what the distribution of scores is like.

5. The normal distribution, skew and z-scores

Characteristics of a normal distribution

  • Symmetrical.
  • Mean, median and mode are all in the center of the distribution.
  • It is asymptotic — the upper and lower tails never actually touch the baseline (the X axis).
Worked example

Industrial effluent from contributing industries to a stream contains bacteria. The total bacteria concentration in that stream, monitored randomly, follows a normal distribution with a mean of 550 counts/100 ml and a standard deviation of 150 counts/100 ml. What is the probability that the total concentration exceeds 700 counts/100 ml?

Skew

When a sample of scores is not normally distributed:

  • Positively skewed: elongated tail at the higher end of the distribution — most scores occur at the low end, with a few scores at the higher end.
  • Negatively skewed: elongated tail at the lower end of the distribution.

Probabilities that statisticians have generated using the normal distribution may not apply well to skewed or otherwise non-normal distributions of data.

Standardization and z-scores

Standardization is the process of converting each score in a distribution to a z-score (also called a standard score). A z-score indicates how far above or below the mean a given score is, in standard deviation units.

Standard error

The standard error is the standard deviation of the sampling distribution of some statistic — a measure of how much random variation we would expect from samples of equal size drawn from the same population. The standard error of the mean provides the average difference between the expected value (e.g. the population mean) and an individual sample mean — how much error we can expect when we say that a sample mean represents the mean of the larger population.

  • The larger the sample, the smaller the standard error.
  • The larger the sample standard deviation, the greater the assumed variation of scores in the population, and consequently the larger the standard error of the mean.

6. Covariance

From variance to covariance:

  • Variance → how does one variable vary?
  • Covariance → how do two variables vary together?

Key idea: covariance tells us the direction of a relationship.

CovarianceMeaning
Positive (+)Both variables tend to increase or decrease together — e.g. rainfall ↑ → streamflow ↑
Negative (−)One tends to increase when the other decreases
Near zeroNo clear linear co-movement

How is covariance calculated?

For two variables X and Y: Cov(X, Y) = Σ(Xi − X̄)(Yi − ŷ) / (n − 1). Think of it in three simple steps:

  1. Find how far each observation is from its mean.
  2. Multiply the deviations of X and Y.
  3. Take their average.

Positive products → positive covariance. Can you think of any examples with negative and zero covariance?

7. Correlation

Correlation describes relations among two or more variables — how two variables relate to each other. One variable may cause variation, or changes, in another variable.

TypeMeaning
Positive correlationValues on the two variables move in the same direction
Negative correlationOn average, as scores on one variable go up, scores on the other go down
Strength / magnitudeRanges from −1 to +1; the most commonly used is the Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient
  • A correlation coefficient of 0 indicates no relationship between the two variables.
  • The closer the coefficient is to −1.00 or +1.00, the stronger the relationship.
  • Perfect correlations are never found in actual research — coefficients generally stay between −.70 and +.70.
  • Rules of thumb: ±.20 weak, .20–.50 moderate, >.50 strong — but take these with a grain of salt: even a “small” correlation between alcohol consumption and liver disease (e.g. +.15) is important.

Requirements for Pearson correlation

  • Both variables must be measured on an interval or ratio scale (continuous variables).
  • Simple Pearson correlations are designed to examine linear relations among variables.

Principal drawback of correlational research

It does not allow for the careful controls necessary for drawing conclusions about causal associations between variables.

A big mistake!

People mistakenly make claims of causation as a function of correlations all the time — very common in advertisements and news stories. Correlation means variation in one variable corresponds with variation in another; causation means variation in one variable causes variation in another. Evidence of a relationship does not necessarily mean a causal relationship — e.g. SAT scores correlate with first-year college GPA (r ≈ .40), but it would be difficult to argue that higher SAT scores cause higher achievement; some other variable or set of variables is probably responsible.

Coefficient of determination

The squared correlation coefficient is known as the coefficient of determination — the percentage of variance explained in each of the two correlated variables by the other variable. Coefficients of determination also do not necessarily indicate a causal relationship.

Covariance vs. correlation — simple takeaway

  • Covariance: do X and Y move together?
  • Correlation: how strongly do they move together?

8. Regression

Regression describes the nature and strength of the relations between variables. The purpose of regression analysis is to make predictions about the value of the dependent variable given certain values of the predictor variable(s).

  • Stronger correlations lead to more accurate predictions.
  • Regression yields a formula for calculating the predicted value of one variable when we know the actual value of other variable(s).
  • Regression of Y on X is different from regression of X on Y.
  • The Pearson correlation coefficient is nothing more than a simple linear regression coefficient that has been standardized.
  • The dependent variable is also called the criterion variable.

Linear bivariate regression

  • Assumption: the two variables are linearly related.
  • Involves a single independent (predictor) variable and a single dependent (outcome) variable.
  • Unlike correlation, regression always designates a predictor variable and a dependent variable.

Worked example — identify the variables yourself

Real-world problem

Tarbela Reservoir inflow forecast. The Indus River System Authority (IRSA) wants to forecast the summer inflow into Tarbela Reservoir so it can plan irrigation releases and issue flood warnings weeks in advance. It has 30 years of records: the average May–July air temperature measured at high-altitude weather stations in the upper Indus catchment, and the corresponding total summer inflow volume recorded at the reservoir. IRSA wants to build a simple linear model — inflow = a + b × temperature — so that from a seasonal temperature forecast alone, it can predict how much water will arrive at Tarbela.

Before reading on, decide for yourself: which variable is which?

1. Which variable is the dependent (outcome) variable — the one we want to predict?

Total summer inflow volume at Tarbela Reservoir. It is the outcome we are trying to predict — in the model inflow = a + b × temperature, inflow is Y.

2. Which variable is the independent (predictor) variable — the one we use to make the prediction?

Average May–July air temperature in the upper Indus catchment. It is the predictor we know (or can forecast) first — in the model, temperature is X.

3. In the model inflow = a + b × temperature, what does the slope b tell us?

The expected change in inflow volume for each 1 °C rise in average temperature. For example, b = 0.8 million acre-feet per °C means a 1 °C warmer season is associated with about 0.8 million acre-feet more inflow. The intercept a is the predicted inflow when temperature is 0 °C.

Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression

OLS minimizes the sum of squared deviations. The line of least squares is the regression line — it represents the predicted values of Y at any given value of X. In any scatter plot, there is only one line that produces the smallest sum of squares. The regression line is defined by its slope and intercept:

  • Regression coefficient (slope): the rate of change in Y with respect to change in X.
  • Intercept: the value of Y when X = 0.

Using the least-squares formulas below, find the slope b and intercept a of the regression line inflow = a + b × temperature. Then use your line to predict the inflow for a season with an average temperature of 19 °C.

Least-squares formulas

b = (n·ΣXY − ΣX·ΣY) / (n·ΣX² − (ΣX)²)   and   a = &Ymacr; − b·X̄

Useful sums to check your arithmetic: ΣX = 165, ΣY = 363.4, ΣXY = 6202.9, ΣX² = 2805, n = 10.

4. What is the slope b of the regression line?

b ≈ 2.51 MAF per °C. Using the formula: b = (10×6202.9 − 165×363.4) / (10×2805 − 165²) = (62029 − 59961) / (28050 − 27225) = 2068 / 825 ≈ 2.51. So each 1 °C warmer season is associated with about 2.5 million acre-feet more inflow.

5. What is the intercept a of the regression line?

a ≈ −5.02 MAF. First find the means: X̄ = 165/10 = 16.5 and &Ymacr; = 363.4/10 = 36.34. Then a = &Ymacr; − b·X̄ = 36.34 − 2.51×16.5 ≈ 36.34 − 41.36 ≈ −5.02. The regression line is therefore inflow ≈ −5.02 + 2.51 × temperature.

6. Using your line, what inflow would you predict for a season with an average temperature of 19 °C?

≈ 42.6 MAF. inflow = −5.02 + 2.51 × 19 = −5.02 + 47.69 ≈ 42.6 MAF. (The actual value in the dataset for 19 °C is 42.7 MAF — very close, because the fit is strong.)

The coefficient of determination R²

R² is a statistical measure that explains the proportion of variance in the dependent variable that is predictable from the independent variable(s).

  • High R²: points close to the regression line.
  • Low R²: points scattered far from the regression line.
  • If R² = 1, the regression line perfectly fits the data; if R² = 0, the independent variable explains none of the variability in the dependent variable.
  • Example: R² = 0.96 means 96% of the variability in exam scores can be explained by the hours studied.

Predicted vs. actual values — residuals

In making predictions about the value of Y, we may be incorrect sometimes. Errors in prediction are known as residuals:

  • Underpredicted → positive residuals.
  • Overpredicted → negative residuals.

Key takeaways

  • Statistics is a workflow: ask → collect → describe → interpret.
  • A parameter describes a population; a statistic is derived from a sample — and samples are not necessarily representative.
  • Describe data by its center (mean, median, mode) and spread (range, variance, standard deviation) — the mean is sensitive to outliers.
  • The normal distribution is symmetrical with mean = median = mode; skewed data needs care, and z-scores express values in standard deviation units.
  • Covariance tells the direction of a relationship; correlation tells its strength (−1 to +1) — but correlation does not imply causation.
  • Regression predicts a dependent variable from predictor(s) using the line of least squares; R² tells how much variance is explained, and prediction errors are residuals.
