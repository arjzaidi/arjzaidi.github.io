2. Data and information

Why do we need water-related information?

  • Monitoring and early warnings
  • Optimal management
  • Equitable allocation
  • Reduced uncertainties
  • Hazard and disaster management

Data versus information

DataInformation
A collection of facts, with no or minimal meaningProcessed data, organized and put into some context
Raw form — unprocessed and unorganizedProvides context, meaning and relevance
Independent of informationDependent on data

In short: data is the building block, while information is the transformed and structured output that enables understanding and decision-making.

The DIKW pyramid

LevelMeaning
DataCollected facts in unorganized form
InformationVisualized data for a specific purpose
KnowledgeConnected information, applied to achieve a goal
WisdomKnowledge applied in action

Collecting data and making it useful actually makes the dataset smaller but concentrates on the main goal — for example, the eucalyptus tree plantation example, or climate change: through prediction models, scientists find out the cause.

3. Concepts and variables

What are concepts?

Concepts are formed through individuals’ mental images and perceptions, and their meanings vary from person to person. They are difficult to measure as they are. Examples: happiness, satisfaction, performance.

What are variables?

A variable is an entity that exhibits variation or change — it can take on different values. Any concept that is capable of measurement is a variable.

Operationalizing concepts

To minimize the level of variation in people’s interpretation, it is essential to operationalize concepts by transforming them into indicators and variables:

  1. Identify indicators.
  2. Convert indicators into variables.
  3. Choices of indicators may vary with the researcher.
  4. There should be a logical link between indicators and their concepts.

Examples: concepts into variables

ConceptIndicatorsVariables
RichWealthIncome; assets converted to value (house, boat, car, investments in $)
High academic achievementAcademic performanceGPA
PowerfulInfluence?
EffectivenessProgram outcomesEnrollments in a program
SatisfactionEmployee feedbackEmployee complaints
Water qualityMicrobial contaminationBacteria count

In qualitative research, measurements and variables do not carry much significance.

4. Types of variables

From the viewpoint of causal relationship

TypeMeaningExample
Independent (change variable)Responsible for bringing about change in a phenomenon; the variable you have control overPrecipitation → runoff; sunny days in London → revenue generated
Dependent (outcome variable)The effects, impacts or consequences of a change in the independent variableRunoff; revenue generated
Extraneous (influencing)Factors in the research environment that may affect the dependent variable but cannot be controlled; they may increase or decrease the strength of the relationshipUncontrolled environmental factors
Intervening (linking)Completes the relationship between the independent and dependent variablesTourism activities linking sunny days in London to revenue
Remember

Extraneous variables are dangerous if not controlled — they may damage a study’s validity. And correlation between two variables does NOT imply causation (e.g. intelligence – education – good grades; workplace stress – physical illness – absenteeism).

From the viewpoint of unit of measurement

Variables can be categorical/qualitative or continuous/quantitative — there is very little difference between the two pairs of terms.

Measurement scales

ScalePropertiesExamples
NominalEach group has a characteristic common to all classified within it; the sequence of listing subgroups is irrelevantGender (male/female); religion (Islam/Christianity/Hindu); tree, house, car, name
OrdinalNominal characteristics plus related subgroups arranged in ascending or descending order; ranked by magnitude but actual magnitude is not quantifiable; distance between categories is not equalIncome (above average / average / below average); age groups (infant / young / old)
IntervalOrdinal plus an arbitrary starting and ending point; equally spaced intervals; not an absolute measurement — 60°C is not twice as hot as 30°CCelsius scale for temperature (0–100°C)
RatioAll properties of an interval scale plus a fixed zero starting point; absolute measurement — 60 cm is twice as long as 30 cm; supports mathematical operationsHeight in cm; flow volume

A simple way to distinguish them: are the values just labels, categories with names, equally spaced numbers, or numbers with a meaningful zero?

Why does measurement matter?

  • It defines the type of analysis.
  • It determines the type of applicable statistical procedures.
  • It shapes the way the data can be interpreted.
  • It determines how the outcomes can be communicated.
  • The style of your report entirely depends on the way variables are measured — the way a question has been asked and its response recorded.

5. Data for hydroinformatics

The water cycle as a data system

Hydroinformatics works with data from the entire water cycle — precipitation, evapotranspiration, soil moisture, runoff, groundwater and water quality — collected through applied observational systems such as remote sensing sensors and Earth observation satellites (e.g. Terra and Aqua). Hydrological sensors monitor water quality and water levels in rainwater, surface water, groundwater, rivers and reservoirs.

As we collect more data, the challenge increases: to utilize detailed data effectively in analysis, modeling and prediction studies. Classic problems relate to data storage, representation, search, communication, visualization, knowledge and discovery.

Trends in hydroinformatics

  • Crowdsourcing — citizen-contributed observations.
  • Automated sensor data retrieval — SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition) and telemetry (automatic measurement and wireless transmission of data from remote sources).
  • Space-based remote sensing — satellite observations of the water cycle.
  • Artificial intelligence systems — machine learning for forecasting and pattern detection.
  • Cloud computing — scalable storage and processing.

For water managers and engineers, big data shows promise in planning optimum water systems, detecting ecosystem changes through big remote sensing and GIS, forecasting and detecting natural and manmade calamities, scheduling irrigations, mitigating environmental pollution, and studying climate change impacts.

6. Why hydroinformatics matters

Why do we need hydroinformatics as water professionals?

  1. To build the adaptive capacity of regions that are prone to water-related disasters.
  2. To track the progress of water-related interventions in natural or urban environments.
  3. To practice multi-stakeholder decision-making and evidence-based implementation.
  4. To bring sustainable development to basin-level management of rivers — the hydroinformatics approach to Integrated River Basin Management (IRBM).
  5. To solve water-related conflicts among stakeholders — for example, hydroinformatics in flood forecasting and flood management.
Think about it

List out the water-related issues in Pakistan: mismanagement, dams, waterlogging, salinity, poor quality groundwater, politics, corruption, inequitable distribution — how could better data and information help address each?

7. Hydroinformatics in action in Pakistan

University projects and startups

  • Smart water sensor — an Arduino-based flow measurement sensor developed at the University of Utah by USPCAS-W students.
  • Soil moisture sensor and database — USPCAS-W students determined soil moisture in soil samples and, according to the crop water requirement (fed into a database), the system tells how much more water is required.
  • Sensor installation for altimetric application at Guddu/Sukkur barrages by USPCAS-W — Indus River water level monitoring in Sindh using satellite radar altimetry.
  • ERFMS Portal by the University of Agriculture Faisalabad.
  • TRIM — The River Indus Monitor by USPCAS-W (trim-uspcasw.vercel.app).

Government initiatives

  • Irrigation Advisory System by PCRWR — combines NASA weather measurements (temperature, wind speed, pressure, solar radiation, humidity) with weather prediction models to create real-time estimates of the water needed by specific crops.
  • Surface and Groundwater Discharge Portal by the Punjab Irrigation Department.
  • Rotational Program and Tail Watercourse by the Punjab Irrigation Department.
  • Pakistan Meteorological Department daily database — including rim stations and the eastern (Indus, Jhelum, Chenab) and western (Kabul) rivers.
  • WAPDA monitoring and telecommunication systems.
  • Plans proposed by the Ministry of Water Resources: flood forecasting and early warning systems; radar networks of PMD (existing + proposed); Meteor Bus telecommunication system of WAPDA; high frequency radio network at western rivers; wireless network for eastern rivers; PCIW’s cross-border hydraulic data sharing plan.

Key takeaways

  • Data is raw and unorganized; information is processed data with context — and the DIKW pyramid climbs from data to information to knowledge to wisdom.
  • Concepts (happiness, satisfaction, water quality) must be operationalized into indicators and variables before they can be measured.
  • Variables are classified by causal role (independent, dependent, extraneous, intervening) and by measurement scale (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio) — and correlation does not imply causation.
  • The measurement scale of a variable defines the analysis, the statistics, the interpretation and the way results are communicated.
  • Hydroinformatics turns water-cycle data into decisions — through crowdsourcing, sensors, remote sensing, AI and cloud computing.
  • In Pakistan, hydroinformatics is already in action — from USPCAS-W student sensors and TRIM to PCRWR’s irrigation advisory system and the Ministry of Water Resources’ flood forecasting plans.
