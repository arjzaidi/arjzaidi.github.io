1. Recap — what is risk?

Definition

Risk is the probability that loss will occur as the result of an adverse event, given the hazard and the vulnerability.

Risk (R) can be determined as a product of hazard (H) and vulnerability (V): R = H × V. It is a “measure of the expected losses due to a hazard event occurring in a given area over a specific time period.”

What is at risk? — the consequences of hazards

  • Personal injury — harm to people exposed to the hazard.
  • Destruction of property — often aggravated by poor building material and maintenance.
  • Damage to the local economy — disruption of businesses, markets and livelihoods.
  • Opportunity loss — the diversion of economic resources to recovery efforts.

2. Risk acceptability

Risks can also be prioritized according to the acceptability of the population at risk. A risk that causes more fatalities or damages each year may still be more acceptable to the exposed population than another, much less dangerous risk — because of the benefits that would be lost if the risk were partially or fully mitigated.

Example

Transportation accidents have caused more fatalities in the United States and worldwide than nuclear power accidents. Yet most people are much more willing to accept the risk of transportation accidents than nuclear accidents — because the loss of automobiles would have a much more personal and profound impact. (Coppola, Emergency Management)

Class discussion

How would you prioritize the risks in your own community according to their acceptability?

3. The risk equation

Risk can be expressed as the probability of losses:

Risk equation

Risk = Hazard × Vulnerability × Amount of elements-at-risk

or, equivalently:

Risk equation (with capacity)

Risk = Hazard × Vulnerability / Capacity

Components of the equation

ComponentMeaning
Hazard Probability of an event with a certain magnitude — defined by its triggering mechanism, type of event, and magnitude (volume, distance, duration, speed, etc.).
Vulnerability Degree of damage — a function of the magnitude of the event and the type of elements at risk.
Amount Quantification of the elements at risk — e.g. replacement costs of buildings and infrastructure, loss of function or economic activities, and number of people.
Capacity Expresses the positive managerial and operational resources and procedures for reducing risk factors — e.g. awareness raising, early warning, and preparedness planning.

The first equation is not only conceptual — it can actually be calculated with spatial data in a GIS to quantify risk, with a focus on (direct) physical, population and economic losses, summed over all possible risks.

4. Hazard risk assessment

Hazard risk assessment is the process by which the threat posed by each identified hazard is investigated. Essentially, a natural hazard risk assessment is a study undertaken to determine the range of possible consequences from a natural hazard.

  • Risk depends on hazard likelihood and hazard consequences.
  • These two factors are constantly changing as a result of increased development, access to new information, changes in climate and community characteristics, etc.
  • High-likelihood / high-consequence hazards are of special consideration; low-likelihood / low-consequence hazards are of least concern — and all hazards fall in between.
  • A comprehensive Risk Management Program can greatly reduce life and property losses.
Remember

For rare yet real hazards, only the knowledge or opinion of experts can provide an insight — for example, technological hazards.

5. Hazard identification and hazard profiling

Hazard identification

Identify all hazards that have affected or could affect the area of focus through:

  • Historical data
  • Brainstorming
  • Scientific analysis
  • Subject matter expertise

Hazard profiling

Hazard profiling is a component of hazard identification where all hazards in a study area are defined:

  • General description of the hazard
  • Location of the hazard and the spatial extent of its effects
  • Duration of an event caused by the hazard
  • Seasonal or time-based patterns followed by the hazard
  • Speed of onset of an actual hazard event
  • Availability of warning for the hazard

6. Vulnerability assessment

Definition

Vulnerability assessment is the process of estimating the vulnerability to potential disaster hazards of specified elements at risk — determining the nature and extent of vulnerability by analyzing the existing conditions and characteristics of an element or system exposed to a particular type of natural hazard event.

Vulnerability to natural hazards generally refers to the characteristics of an element exposed to a hazard — the extent to which a community’s structure, service or environment is likely to be damaged or disrupted by the impact of a hazard. Risk vulnerability looks at four factors: social, environmental, physical and economic vulnerability.

Vulnerability curves

Vulnerability assessment identifies the relationship between the hazard and the element at risk. Vulnerability is evaluated as the interaction between the intensity of the hazard and the type of element at risk, making use of so-called vulnerability curves.

How to do a vulnerability assessment?

  • Which are the most important elements at risk?
  • Which methods for vulnerability assessment could be used?
  • What are the data requirements?
  • What are the aggravating factors of vulnerability?
  • What are the examples?
Aggravating factors

Degraded environment, urbanization, population explosion, etc.

Data requirements

Base data from which information related to risk can be derived — e.g. soil data, DEM, land use / land cover, social and economic data, hydrological conditions, building footprints, building heights, and road networks. Lack of base data may impose problems in modeling risk.

7. Risk assessment — estimation and evaluation

Risk assessment

Risk Assessment = Risk Estimation + Risk Evaluation

For different hazards there are different methods for loss estimation and different data requirements.

Risk analysis methodology

  • Both qualitative and quantitative methods exist.
  • Various computer-based models are available.
  • Risk assessment outcomes depend on the quality and availability of data.

8. Qualitative and quantitative risk analysis

Qualitative risk analysis

An analysis which uses word form, descriptive or numeric rating scales to describe the magnitude of potential consequences and the likelihood that those consequences will occur — resulting in a qualitative value of the risk, e.g. High Risk, Moderate Risk, Low Risk, etc.

Qualitative risk matrices classify combinations of likelihood and consequence into risk categories — for example:

CategoryMeaning
AVery High Risk
BHigh Risk
CMedium Risk
DLow Risk

Quantitative risk analysis

An analysis which is based on numerical values of the probability, vulnerability, and consequences — resulting in a numerical value of the risk, e.g. Risk = $5,000, etc.

Quantitative analysis works with return periods and probabilities:

Return period (RP)Probability of hazard per year
2 years1/2 = 0.5
10 years1/10 = 0.1
50 years1/50 = 0.02

Risk curves

For a number of hazard scenarios, the consequences are plotted against the temporal probability of occurrence of the hazard events. A curve is fitted through these points — the so-called risk curve — and the area below the curve presents the total risk.

Note

Multi-hazard risk assessment is very complicated — care should be taken to evaluate also the interrelations between hazards (e.g. domino effects, such as a landslide damming a river and causing a flood).

9. Elements at risk

Definition

Elements at risk are all objects, persons, animals, activities and processes that may be adversely affected by hazardous phenomena, in a particular area, either directly or indirectly.

This includes:

  • Buildings
  • Facilities
  • Population
  • Livestock
  • Economic activities
  • Public services
  • Environment

Elements-of-risk matrix

Each element at risk has a specific vulnerability to a specific hazard. The elements-of-risk matrix links hazard types to the elements at risk and the sources of information used to map them:

Hazard typeElement at riskSources (bold = primary source)
Earthquake Buildings — building type, structure type, material, height, use, shape, age Register · Map · Field · Image
Lifelines Register · Map · Field · Image
Population Register · Map · Field · Image
Essential facilities Register · Map · Field · Image
Flood Buildings, lifelines, population, essential facilities Register · Map · Field · Image

Exposure to hazard — the degree to which elements at risk coincide spatially and temporally with the hazard — determines which elements are actually affected when a hazard event occurs.

10. Reducing disaster risk

Three major components are to be considered in disaster management:

  • Hazard
  • Vulnerability
  • Capacity / amount of losses

Therefore, disaster risk can be reduced through systematic efforts of:

  • Reducing hazard exposure,
  • Lessening vulnerability and/or
  • Developing a greater capacity to face these disasters

— which may reduce the impact of a hazard.

Key takeaways

  • Risk is the probability that loss will occur as the result of an adverse event, given the hazard and the vulnerability — R = H × V.
  • The risk equation is Risk = Hazard × Vulnerability × Amount of elements at risk (or / Capacity) — and it can be calculated with spatial data in a GIS.
  • Hazard risk assessment investigates the threat posed by each identified hazard; risk depends on hazard likelihood and hazard consequences, which are constantly changing.
  • Hazard profiling defines all hazards in a study area — description, location and extent, duration, seasonal patterns, speed of onset, and warning availability.
  • Vulnerability assessment identifies the relationship between hazard intensity and the type of element at risk, using vulnerability curves.
  • Risk assessment = risk estimation + risk evaluation; methods are qualitative (High / Moderate / Low Risk) or quantitative (numerical values of probability, vulnerability and consequences).
  • Elements at risk include buildings, facilities, population, livestock, economic activities, public services and the environment.
  • Disaster risk can be reduced by reducing hazard exposure, lessening vulnerability and developing capacity.
