1. Hazard and risk

Quick quiz

A hazard is a source of danger that may or may not lead to a disaster. Can you explain, with an example, why every hazard that materialises does not necessarily become a disaster?

“Hazards are always prevalent, but a hazard becomes a disaster only when the frequency or likelihood of a hazard and the vulnerability of the community increases the risk of being severely affected.”

Hazard and risk

  • Each hazard carries an associated risk.
  • Risk depends on the likelihood of a hazard leading to an actual disaster, and the consequences of that disaster.
  • When the response requirements for an emergency event exceed the capabilities of available emergency services, the event is classified as a disaster.
  • Knowledge of risk is essential for preparedness, planning and mitigation activities.

Risk: definition

Definition

Risk is a measure of the expected losses due to a hazard event occurring in a given area over a specific time period — a function of the probability of a particular hazardous event and the losses each would cause.

UNISDR

“The potential disaster losses, in lives, health status, livelihoods, assets and services, which could occur to a particular community or a society over some specified future time period.”

Risk depends on

  • The nature of the hazard (its probability and intensity).
  • The vulnerability of the elements which are affected.
  • The capacity to recover.
  • The economic value of those elements.

Disaster risk management includes all measures which reduce disaster-related losses of life, property or assets by either reducing the hazard or the vulnerability of the elements at risk.

Hazard and vulnerability meet

A disaster happens when a hazard impacts on a vulnerable population and causes damage, casualties and disruption — disaster occurs only when hazards and vulnerability meet. A hazard is disastrous only when it affects people, their properties and activities. For example, an earthquake in an uninhabited desert cannot be considered a disaster, no matter how strong the intensities produced.

2. Vulnerability

Definition

Definition

Vulnerability is the extent to which a community, structure, services or geographic area is likely to be damaged or disrupted by the impact of a particular hazard, on account of their nature, construction, and proximity to hazardous terrains or a disaster-prone area.

Types of vulnerability

TypeMeaning
Physical vulnerabilityWho and what may be damaged or destroyed by a natural hazard — based on the physical condition of people and elements at risk.
Socio-economic vulnerabilityThe socio-economic condition of the people affected by a disaster — for example, poor people living on the coast cannot afford strong concrete houses and lose their shelters in every cyclone.

Vulnerability is determined by physical, social, economic and environmental factors that increase the susceptibility of a community to the impact of hazards.

Examples of vulnerability

  • Poor design and construction of buildings.
  • Inadequate protection of assets.
  • Lack of public awareness.
  • Limited recognition of risks and preparedness measures, and lack of proper management.

IPCC definition

The IPCC (Turner II et al., 2003) defines vulnerability as a function of:

  • Exposure — people, property, systems or other elements present in hazard zones that are subject to potential losses.
  • Sensitivity — of a system to changes in climate.
  • Adaptive capacity — the ability of the system to adapt to changes in climate.

Under this framework, a highly vulnerable system is one that is highly sensitive to modest changes in climate.

Vulnerability assessment and mapping

  • Vulnerability is different for different hazards, elements at risk, environments, conditions and locations.
  • Assessment tools include vulnerability indices, vulnerability curves, fragility curves and vulnerability tables.
  • Vulnerability maps show where vulnerable people and assets are concentrated — for example, district-level poverty and human development maps used in Pakistan.
  • Risk and vulnerability indices are published by the World Bank (Hotspots), UNDP (Disaster Risk Index), the World Economic Forum, IFRC (World Disasters Report) and CRED.

3. Capacity

Definition

Definition

Capacity is the combination of resources, means and strengths which exist in communities to enable them to cope with, withstand, prepare for, prevent, mitigate, or quickly recover from a disaster.

Capacity to respond and recover

  • The ability of a community to respond to and recover from a hazard in order to reduce its potential impact.
  • The positive managerial capabilities of individuals, households and communities to confront the threat of disasters — through awareness raising, early warning and preparedness planning.

Types of capacity

TypeMeaning
Physical capacityCapacity to salvage things after disasters, or skills that may lead to new opportunities — e.g. salvaging from destroyed homes or farms, or family members with skills that enable them to find employment if they migrate.
Socio-economic capacityCapacity to recover soon because of good socio-economic conditions — e.g. wealthy people live in safer areas, build with stronger materials, and can cope even when everything is destroyed.

4. UNISDR terminology

Key terms as defined by the UN International Strategy for Disaster Reduction (2009 terminology):

TermDefinition
RiskThe combination of the probability of an event and its negative consequences.
Acceptable riskThe level of potential losses that a society or community considers acceptable given existing social, economic, political, cultural, technical and environmental conditions.
Disaster riskThe potential disaster losses, in lives, health status, livelihoods, assets and services, which could occur to a particular community or society over some specified future time period.
CapacityThe combination of all the strengths, attributes and resources available within a community, society or organisation that can be used to achieve agreed goals.
VulnerabilityThe characteristics and circumstances of a community, system or asset that make it susceptible to the damaging effects of a hazard.
ExposurePeople, property, systems or other elements present in hazard zones that are subject to potential losses.
PreventionThe outright avoidance of adverse impacts of hazards and related disasters.
MitigationThe lessening or limitation of the adverse impacts of hazards and related disasters.
PreparednessThe knowledge and capacities developed to effectively anticipate, respond to, and recover from the impacts of likely, imminent or current hazard events.
ResponseThe provision of emergency services and public assistance during or immediately after a disaster to save lives, reduce health impacts, ensure public safety, and meet basic subsistence needs.
RecoveryThe restoration, and improvement where appropriate, of facilities, livelihoods and living conditions of disaster-affected communities, including efforts to reduce disaster risk factors.
ResilienceThe ability of a system, community or society exposed to hazards to resist, absorb, accommodate to and recover from the effects of a hazard in a timely and efficient manner — the ability to “bounce back” after adversity.

5. Disaster versus emergency

Disaster characteristics (Cees van Westen)

A disaster is:

  • An extreme phenomenon of large intensity (e.g. earthquake intensity, water depth).
  • Of limited duration (from seconds to months, but defined in time).
  • Occurring at a certain location — the spatial component is very important in this course.
  • Involving a complex interplay between physical and human systems.
  • Causing loss of lives and threats to public health, as well as physical damage, and disruption of livelihood systems and society.
  • Exceeding local capacities and resources, requiring outside assistance to cope.

What is an emergency?

A disaster is an emergency in which local authorities cannot cope with the impact or scale of the hazard, and therefore the event is managed outside the affected communities.

TypeExamples
DisastersEarthquakes, hurricanes, floods
Non-disaster emergenciesLocal floods, local epidemics, transport or industrial accidents, environmental pollution

An “incident” is a minor situation; an “emergency” a more serious situation; a “disaster” a yet more serious situation; and a “catastrophe” the most serious situation of all. Accidents are usual events; disasters are unusual events. Disasters are distinguished from emergencies by the greater level of response required — for example, definitions such as “at least 100 human deaths, at least 100 human injuries, or at least US$1 million in economic damages”.

Is it a disaster?

Discuss: When you become ill and cannot work anymore? When a football player’s injury loses his team the world championship? When does a car accident become a disaster (US: ~US$230 billion/year, 2.9 million injuries, ~43,000 casualties)? The death of 2,974 people in the 9/11 attacks? COVID-19? The 2008 financial crisis? The IFRC has argued that HIV/AIDS matches the UN definition of a disaster.

6. Hazard profiling

Hazard profiling identifies all hazards that have affected or could affect the area of focus through:

  • Historical records
  • Brainstorming
  • Scientific analysis
  • Subject matter expertise (for rare yet real hazards, e.g. technological hazards)

Hazard identification is the foundation of all emergency management activities. A hazard profile includes:

  • A general description of the hazard.
  • The location of the hazard and the spatial extent of its effects.
  • The duration of an event caused by the hazard.
  • Seasonal or time-based patterns followed by the hazard.
  • The speed of onset of an actual hazard event.
  • The availability of warning for the hazard.

7. Disaster risk management

What is disaster risk management?

Disaster risk management (DRM) is the systematic process of using administrative directives, organisations and operational skills and capacities to implement strategies, policies and improved coping capacities to lessen the adverse impacts of hazards and the possibility of a disaster. It aims to avoid, lessen or transfer the adverse effects of hazards through activities and measures for prevention, mitigation and preparedness.

Disaster risk reduction (DRR) is the concept and practice of reducing disaster risks through systematic efforts to analyse and manage the causal factors of disasters — including reduced exposure to hazards, lessened vulnerability of people and property, wise management of land and the environment, and improved preparedness for adverse events.

The historical context

  • Emergency management has ancient roots, but organised attempts at dealing with disasters did not occur until much later in modern history.
  • Emergency management was once all about response to disasters and social and economic recovery; now it also focuses on risk analysis, disaster reduction and mitigation.
  • Disaster management is now a proactive and multidisciplinary approach.

Why are disasters increasing worldwide?

Two broad reasons: an increase in vulnerability, and factors leading to a higher number of hazardous events.

Increased vulnerability is driven by:

  • Rapid increase of the world population.
  • Large urbanisation rates (related to population pressure).
  • Human settlements in areas susceptible to natural hazards.
  • Most of the world’s population living in hazardous regions — coastal zones or seismically active regions.
  • Development of highly sensitive technologies and the growing susceptibility of modern industrial societies to infrastructure breakdowns.
  • Developing countries with lower insurance coverage.

The frequency of destructive events related to atmospheric extremes (floods, droughts, cyclones, landslides) is also increasing, and climate change is negatively related to the occurrence of natural disasters.

Risk reduction

There are three major components in disaster management: hazard, vulnerability and capacity. Disaster risk can therefore be reduced through systematic efforts of:

  • Reducing hazard exposure,
  • Lessening vulnerability, and
  • Developing greater capacity to face these disasters.

Determinants of risk: five elements of a community

ElementIncludes
PeopleIndividuals and communities
PropertyInfrastructure, possessions and assets; public, private and cultural
ServicesGovernment and non-government, commercial and voluntary
LivelihoodUrban and rural, formal and informal
EnvironmentAir, water and soil; urban and rural, built and natural

Each of the above has specific vulnerability to a specific hazard.

Risk analysis steps

  1. Identify a hazard.
  2. Identify the communities exposed to that hazard.
  3. Predict the consequences or impact of the hazard on the communities exposed.
  4. Determine the vulnerabilities of the five elements of these communities.
  5. Identify the capacities of the communities to respond to that hazard.

The purpose of risk analysis is to plan for public safety by developing hazard reduction plans, vulnerability reduction plans and emergency preparedness plans. Risk assessment can be qualitative or quantitative, uses various computer-based models, and its outcomes depend on the quality and availability of data. High-likelihood / high-consequence hazards are of special consideration.

The disaster management cycle

PhaseMeaning
MitigationSustained actions to reduce or eliminate long-term risk to people and property from hazards and their effects.
PreparednessBuilding the emergency management function to respond effectively to, and recover from, any hazard.
ResponseEmergency operations to save lives and property — reducing the hazard to acceptable levels, evacuating potential victims, providing food, water, shelter and medical care, and restoring critical public services.
RecoveryRebuilding communities so that individuals, businesses and governments can function on their own, return to normal life, and protect against future hazards.

The phases often overlap — there is no clearly defined boundary where one phase ends and another begins. Successful emergency management coordinates activities in all four phases.

Risk transfer

Risk transfer is the process of formally or informally shifting the financial consequences of particular risks from one party to another. Disaster risk transfer mechanisms (e.g. insurance, reinsurance, calamity funds, emergency and reconstruction loans) are a component of both disaster management and disaster risk reduction — for example, adequate insurance premiums can promote and encourage disaster risk reduction measures in the insured elements.

Key takeaways

  • Risk = the probability of a hazard event × its consequences; it depends on the hazard, vulnerability, capacity and economic value of the elements at risk.
  • A disaster occurs only when hazards and vulnerability meet — a hazard in an uninhabited area is not a disaster.
  • Vulnerability (physical and socio-economic) and capacity (physical and socio-economic) are the two sides of the same coin — poverty increases vulnerability and reduces capacity.
  • A disaster is an emergency that exceeds local coping capacity; disasters are distinguished from emergencies by the greater level of response required.
  • Hazard profiling — describing hazards by location, duration, patterns, speed of onset and warning — is the foundation of emergency management.
  • DRM reduces risk by reducing hazard exposure, lessening vulnerability and developing capacity, through the cycle of mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery.
