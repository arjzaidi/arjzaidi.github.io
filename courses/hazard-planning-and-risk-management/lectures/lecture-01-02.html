Learning outcomes of the course

After completing this course, you will be able to:

  • LEARN the concepts of hazards and disasters (H&D) management.
  • UNDERSTAND the causes and characteristics of natural hazards and related disasters.
  • DISTINGUISH between different phases of H&D management — mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery.
  • APPLY hazard management knowledge to recognize, assess and deal with disaster risks.
  • LEARN meaningful strategies for disaster prevention, mitigation, response and recovery.
  • USE GIS and satellite data in different phases of hazard management.
  • UNDERSTAND and/or DEVELOP disaster management plans.

2. What is a hazard?

A hazard is a dangerous condition or event that threatens, or has the potential for causing, injury to life or damage to property or the environment.

Formal definitions

UNISDR

“A potentially damaging physical event, phenomenon, or human activity that may cause the loss of life or injury, property damage, social and economic disruption, or environmental degradation.”

UNDRO (1991)

“The probability of occurrence within a specified period of time and within a given area of a potentially damaging phenomenon.”

UNISDR is the United Nations International Strategy for Disaster Reduction; UNDRO is the United Nations Disaster Relief Organization.

Hazard categories

Hazards fall into two main categories, with some hazards combining both:

CategoryCauseExamples
NaturalNatural processes or phenomena within the earth system (lithosphere, hydrosphere, biosphere, atmosphere)Earthquake, volcanic eruption, tsunami, landslide, flood, drought, cyclone
Manmade (technological)Human negligence or failure, including infrastructure failureWar, industrial accidents, oil spills, chemical and nuclear accidents
Socio-naturalBoth natural and manmade causes acting togetherA flood caused by both excessive rain and land-use change

Examples of hazards

GroupExamples
Geological hazardsEarthquake, volcanic eruption, tsunami, landslide
Water & climatic hazardsFloods, droughts, cyclones
Environmental hazardsEnvironmental pollution, deforestation, forest fire
Manmade or technological hazardsWar, industrial accidents, oil spills

Note: these categories are not mutually exclusive — for example, a forest fire may also be an accident-related hazard.

What do you think about global warming?

  • Is global warming a hazard? If so, what type — environmental, manmade, or something else?
  • Global warming and temperature rise may cause many other hazards — for example: glacier retreat, floods, droughts, forest fires, hurricanes and cyclones.

3. The hazard spectrum (Jones, 1993)

Not every hazard is purely natural or purely manmade. The hazard spectrum recognises two intermediate classes:

ClassMeaningExamples
Quasi-naturalA natural hazard caused or triggered by human activityLandslide due to road cutting, dust storm due to overgrazing, flood due to dam failure
Na-techNatural hazards triggering technological disastersThe 2011 Tohoku earthquake and tsunami triggering the meltdowns at TEPCO’s Fukushima Daiichi nuclear power station
Think about it

If human activity has caused a natural hazard, can we still call it “natural”? Where would you place climate-change-amplified floods or droughts on this spectrum?

4. What is a disaster?

The meaning of the word ‘disaster’

The meaning of the word has changed through time (E. L. Quarantelli):

  • In early usage it referred to unfavorable or negative effects, usually of a personal nature, resulting from a star or a planet — “we make guilty of our disasters the sun, the moon and stars” (Shakespeare, King Lear, Act I, Scene 2, 1605).
  • Later, the word was applied to major physical disturbances such as earthquakes and floods — what were traditionally known as Acts of God.
  • The term natural later substituted for supernatural.
  • In recent decades, the notion of “Acts of Men” has joined “Acts of God” — some social scientists describe disasters as manifestations of the vulnerabilities of social systems.

So the perceived source of the problem has shifted over time: from the stars, to God, to nature, to men and women, and now to society — the interactive effect of nature and society.

Characteristics of a disaster

  • A sudden, adverse or unfortunate extreme event causing great damage to human beings as well as plants and animals.
  • Either natural or manmade; from intense to diffuse; from predictable to unpredictable.
  • Disasters occur rapidly, instantaneously and indiscriminately (at random, haphazardly, by chance).
  • Severity varies and can be measured in absolute terms: deaths, economic damages, and number of people affected.
  • Impact depends on the capability of the area to absorb the destructive effects — an event causing disaster at the local level may not be impacting at the regional level.

Formal definitions

United Nations (UNISDR)

“A serious disruption of the functioning of a community or a society involving widespread human, material, economic or environmental losses and impacts, which exceeds the ability of the affected community or society to cope using its own resources.”

Blaikie (1994)

“A disaster occurs when a significant number of vulnerable people experience a hazard and suffer severe damage and/or disruption of their livelihood system in such a way that the recovery is unlikely without external aid.”

CRED

“A situation or event which overwhelms local capacity, necessitating a request to national or international level for external assistance; an unforeseen and often sudden event that causes great damage, destruction and human suffering.”

CRED is the Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters, whose database is EM-DAT.

Key takeaways

  • A hazard is a potentially damaging event or condition; a disaster is what happens when a hazard exceeds the affected community’s ability to cope.
  • Hazards are natural, manmade, or socio-natural — and the categories are not mutually exclusive.
  • The hazard spectrum adds quasi-natural (human-triggered natural hazards) and na-tech (natural hazards triggering technological disasters).
  • The idea of a disaster has evolved from “Acts of God” to “Acts of Men” — disasters are now understood as the interaction of nature and society.
  • Disaster severity is measured by deaths, economic damages and people affected — and impact depends on the capacity of the area to absorb the effects.
