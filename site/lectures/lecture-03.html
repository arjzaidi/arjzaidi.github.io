1. The World Disasters Report (IFRC)

The IFRC commissions an annual independent publication called the World Disasters Report. Each year, experts present a body of research that examines challenges, trends and innovations around a specific humanitarian theme.

World Disasters Report 2026

The 2026 report warns that harmful information has become a de facto humanitarian crisis — undermining access to aid, eroding trust, destabilising social cohesion and increasing risks for staff, volunteers and communities. No longer a peripheral communications issue, harmful information is now an operational and strategic challenge affecting acceptance, safety and principled humanitarian action.

The report brings together insights from nearly 100 contributors and 60 organisations, including over 30 National Red Cross and Red Crescent Societies. Across eight chapters, it shows how harmful information disrupts preparedness, response and recovery — and offers practical recommendations to strengthen information resilience, safeguard humanitarian principles and rebuild trust.

Three related concepts are central to the 2026 report:

  • Misinformation — false or inaccurate information shared without intent to deceive.
  • Disinformation — deliberately false information spread to deceive.
  • Hate speech — damaging narratives that dehumanise groups and destabilise communities.

World Risk Report (Bündnis Entwicklung Hilft & IFHV)

The World Risk Report 2024, published by Bündnis Entwicklung Hilft in partnership with the Institute for International Law of Peace and Armed Conflict (IFHV) at Ruhr University Bochum, ranks 193 countries on their risk of experiencing a disaster or their vulnerability to extreme natural events such as earthquakes, tsunamis, floods and drought. The 2024 findings show that the Philippines, Indonesia and India have the highest overall disaster risk. The report also examines how crises such as pandemics, extreme weather and conflict interact with and amplify one another, and looks at the global water crisis and the catastrophes–conflict nexus.

The World Risk Report 2023 focused on Disaster Risk and Diversity, analysing how disaster risk correlates with dimensions of diversity — age, gender, health, sexual orientation and social background — and outlining pathways towards more inclusive disaster management.

2. Climate Reports and the UN Climate Process

UN Climate Reports

The United Nations publishes key reports on climate impacts and solutions, including the UNFCCC NDC Synthesis Report, the UNEP Production Gap Report and the UNEP Adaptation Gap Report.

IPCC — Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change

The IPCC prepares comprehensive Assessment Reports on knowledge about climate change, its causes, potential impacts and response options. It also produces Special Reports (assessments of a specific issue) and Methodology Reports (practical guidelines, e.g. for greenhouse gas inventories).

  • SREX (2012) — Managing the Risks of Extreme Events and Disasters to Advance Climate Change Adaptation: a landmark report linking disaster risk management and climate change adaptation.
  • AR6 Synthesis Report (March 2023) — the latest assessment cycle: Working Group I (physical science, Aug 2021), Working Group II (impacts and adaptation, Feb 2022), Working Group III (mitigation, Apr 2022), and the Synthesis Report (Mar 2023).

Governing bodies: COP, CMP and CMA

The Convention, the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement established three governing bodies:

  • COP — for the Convention
  • CMP — for the Kyoto Protocol
  • CMA — for the Paris Agreement

The Bureau of the COP, CMP and CMA supports the work of the governing bodies, the UNFCCC secretariat provides organisational support and technical expertise, and two permanent subsidiary bodies — the SBSTA and the SBI — assist them.

Read more: how the COP evolved (timeline)
  • 1987 — Montreal Protocol: a historic global agreement to stop producing CFCs that damage the ozone layer.
  • 1992 — UNFCCC established and ratified by 197 countries; it founded the annual COP forum.
  • 2005 — Kyoto Protocol becomes legally binding (after Russia became the 55th ratifying country).
  • 2015 — Paris Agreement signed, aimed at all nations.
Latest conferences

COP29 took place in Baku, Azerbaijan (2024). The next UN Climate Change Conference, COP31, will take place in Antalya, Türkiye, from 9 to 20 November 2026.

3. EM-DAT — The International Disaster Database

EM-DAT is maintained by the CRED (Centre for Research on the Epidemiology of Disasters). It provides geographical, temporal, human and economic information on disasters at the country level — from 1900 to the present.

Classification of disasters

EM-DAT classifies disasters into two main groups — natural and technological:

Read more: definitions of the natural hazard groups
  • Geophysical — a hazard originating from solid earth (used interchangeably with geological hazard).
  • Meteorological — short-lived, micro- to meso-scale extreme weather and atmospheric conditions lasting from minutes to days.
  • Hydrological — caused by the occurrence, movement and distribution of surface and subsurface freshwater and saltwater.
  • Climatological — long-lived, meso- to macro-scale atmospheric processes ranging from intra-seasonal to multi-decadal climate variability.
  • Biological — caused by exposure to living organisms and their toxic substances, or vector-borne diseases they may carry.
  • Extra-terrestrial — caused by asteroids, meteoroids and comets passing near Earth, and changes in interplanetary conditions affecting the Earth's magnetosphere, ionosphere and thermosphere.

Disaster profiles and trends

EM-DAT offers pre-designed disaster profiles — summaries of events from 1900, including the top 10 disasters, filterable by period, disaster type, subgroup and continent. Its trends tool plots indicators over time (e.g. number of disasters, deaths, and total damage scaled to 2014 US$), by continent and disaster type.

Discussion — what do the trends show?

Looking at the EM-DAT trend charts for 1900–2015 — deaths caused by natural disasters, the total number of reported disasters, total damage, and technological disasters — any comment on these trends? What might explain the patterns you observe?

EM-DAT inclusion criteria

When is an event recorded in EM-DAT?

EM-DAT includes all disasters from 1900 until the present, conforming to at least one of the following criteria:

  • 10 or more people reported killed
  • 100 people or more reported affected
  • declaration of a state of emergency
  • call for international assistance

Note: people killed includes those confirmed dead and those missing and presumed dead. People affected are those requiring immediate assistance during a period of emergency (basic survival needs such as food, water, shelter, sanitation and immediate medical assistance). People reported injured or homeless are aggregated with those affected to produce a ‘total number of people affected’.

4. How Risk Develops

A hazard is a danger that a disaster event may develop. A hazard may cause a disaster to a vulnerable society. A disaster is a function of the risk process.

Risk

Risk results from the combination of:

  • exposure to a hazard,
  • conditions of vulnerability, and
  • insufficient capacity or measures to reduce the potential negative consequences of risk.

A disaster happens when a hazard impacts a vulnerable population and causes damages, casualties and disruptions. A hazard is disastrous only when it affects people, their properties and activities.

Example

An earthquake in an uninhabited desert cannot be considered a disaster, no matter how strong the intensities produced. Disaster occurs only when hazards and vulnerability meet.

Hazard + Community → Threat to public safety

Key takeaways

  • Reliable information and public trust are increasingly essential for effective, safe and inclusive humanitarian response — harmful information is now a humanitarian challenge in its own right.
  • The UN climate process has three governing bodies: COP (Convention), CMP (Kyoto Protocol) and CMA (Paris Agreement).
  • EM-DAT is the international disaster database: it classifies disasters as natural or technological and records events meeting at least one of four inclusion criteria.
  • Risk = exposure to a hazard + conditions of vulnerability + insufficient capacity to reduce negative consequences.
  • Disaster occurs only when hazards and vulnerability meet.
